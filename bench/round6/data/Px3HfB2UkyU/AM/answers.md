Because they are digital: many identical copies run on different hardware, each reads a different bit of the internet, works out how it would change its connection strengths, and the copies average those changes, so each copy benefits from all copies' experience (1,000 copies = 1,000x the experience). 3. [18:22]-[22:33], handwired bird recognizer. Layer 1: little bits of edge at different locations, orientations and scales. Layer 2: small combinations of edges, e.g. two edges meeting (potential beak), edges forming a circle (potential eye). Layer 3: combinations of those in the right spatial relationship: possible bird head (beak + eye), possible foot, possible wing tip. Layer 4: fires "bird" when it sees a bunch of possible heads, wing tips and feet together. He calls it a simplified caricature.

7. [32:37]-[37:47]. Words in the context are converted into activity in many learned feature detectors (e.g. cat becomes animate, furry, whiskers, claws, domestic... thousands of features); that feature set is the word's meaning for the net, the words themselves are then discarded. The features of all context words interact over many layers to predict the features of the next word (trained by self-supervised next-word prediction); not a lookup table like old autocomplete. Ambiguity: his example word is "may" (no capitals): a woman's name, a month, or a modal verb (as in would/should). Initially the net activates an average mish-mash of all three; later layers look at surrounding words (e.g. between "April" and "June") and boost month features, suppress the others. He also gives "death" as a shades-of-meaning example (hospital, battle, car accident, miscarriage contexts), but the several-meanings example is "may".

Self-preservation is not built in; it is derived as a sub-goal to achieve other goals.
