# Answers

1. CASP (transcript [52:03]-[52:48]): a community-wide assessment held every 2 years. Teams are given the amino acid sequences of about 100 proteins and try to fold them computationally. The true structures were already determined in labs but not yet public, and serve as the gold standard. A score that measures prediction accuracy is used; a score over 90 would be expected to count as a solution. (Saw: transcript only; no frame.)

2. [16:47]-[17:05] and [20:48]-[21:02]: AlphaGo was first shown 100,000 games played by strong amateurs and made to mimic the human players, then through reinforcement learning played many millions of games against different versions of itself, learning from its errors. The Lee Sedol version was trained on human data (millions of expert games per [20:48]); only the later AlphaZero dropped human data. On move 37 ([17:37]-[17:56]): AlphaGo itself put the probability that a human would have played it at 1 in 10,000; commentators almost unanimously said no human would have chosen it.

3. [56:04]-[56:30] and [56:50]-[57:37]: first CASP entry, AlphaFold was most accurate on 25 of 43 proteins in the hardest category, and beat the second team by nearly 50%. Still a disappointment because it was far from solving the problem in a way a biologist could use: prediction quality varied, was no more useful than previous methods, and the data was not good enough for practical use; they were "the best in the world at a problem the world's not good at." (Wording of "first time" is inferred from sequence; the year is not stated in the transcript.)

4. [50:20]-[50:59]: chess and Go offer effectively unlimited data (play as many games as compute allows); protein folding has a small, fixed dataset from half a century of slow lab experiments that take months or years per structure, and sometimes a structure can never be determined.

5. [75:15]-[75:58]: after CASP14 they decided to fold every protein in existence and release the predictions openly to everyone (rather than build a submission service), with easy access. Released structures of 200 million proteins.
